It was a moonless January night. Rain fell in a fine, unbroken descent — not to cleanse, but to seep and remain, darkening trunks to ink and softening the ground to treachery. We left the main road and entered the forest along a raw track of mud and ruts, the car dipping into shallow holes filled with brown water that fractured our headlights into trembling gold.

The trees did not line the road; they gathered around it. Pines rose straight and dark, birches gleamed like pale bone, upper branches lost in wet obscurity. The forest did not threaten. It simply stood very near, as if listening, vaguely sentient — a margin the night had left open.

The house appeared, a solitary pane of amber trembling in the rain. Smoke thinned from the chimney. It loomed quietly, aware of our approach.

The little white dog waited beneath the veranda, solemn in his age and deafness, a thin veil shading his old eyes. Once from a southern land of sun and stone, he now belonged here among wet pines and clay soil. He approached slowly, nails ticking faintly against the threshold.

Inside, warmth gathered unevenly. The tiled floor remained cold beneath socks. Heat lived near the fire, near the long wooden table, near the stove. From one spot, you could see everything: flames in the hearth, the dark television screen, pots simmering on the stove, heavy chairs set in their usual places, the forest pressing close outside.

Porcelain hens and painted roosters stood above the cupboards, overseeing the set table with domestic vigilance. Icons hung along the walls, a cross and Communion photographs alongside older family portraits gazing from another era, silent and unyielding.

We removed our coats. My father brought in wine. My stepmother moved easily through the kitchen. My little sister eased onto the banquette beneath the large, solemn clock, which presided over the room with a low, settled gravity. Its pendulum swung, absorbing hours and years. It seemed less to measure time than to tend it. Occasionally it needed recalibration by hand, a deliberate gesture reminding us that time here was neither automatic nor given, but coaxed into order.

On the table, the food lay in abundance. Sledzie, silver and glistening under oil and onion. Pasztet held its shape in firm slices. Kielbasa coiled dark and dense. Pickled ogorki, crisp and glinting beside thick-cut bread and pale, smooth butter. Candles burned steadily, like tiny sentinels keeping watch, their flames swaying as shadows passed, dripping wax in slow, molten threads.

Conversation began as it always does — roads, weather, appointments, small inconveniences of the body. It moved lightly at first, weaving through the space between us — touching, retreating, returning. We talked of Vilnius, where I was headed next. My sister spoke about her swimming training — early mornings, quiet laps before school. Someone asked about her next competition.

My step-grandmother presided with composed authority — attentive, efficient, generous in action. There was something quietly grand in the way she hosted — a careful tending, making sure everything remained as it ought. My step-grandfather spoke sparingly, but when he did, the remark landed gently and widened the room. Sometimes he seemed almost elsewhere, as though listening to something just beyond the walls.

As the evening deepened, I asked about something I had half-heard years ago. The conversation drifted, not forward, but to the edges of memory. Stories surfaced — of war, occupation, shortages, quiet movements at night. Some belonged to those at the table; others came from parents, neighbours, or people long gone. A few had been recited so often they had the smoothness of tale; others frayed at the edges, half-remembered, interrupted, corrected.

They emerged between gestures — a hand reaching for bread, another brushing crumbs from the cloth. One memory leaned against another. They spoke briefly of the old dog — how he might not see another spring. My father mentioned his upcoming work trip. The fire shifted; a log settled inward with a muted hiss. Beneath the table, the dog slept.

Nothing in the room grew solemn. Instead, it deepened. The air seemed to acquire layers, other evenings folding into this one without friction. As though we were seated not at the beginning of something, but in the middle of an ongoing passage. It felt less like we were creating a new night than stepping into a pattern already woven. Gradually, the evening became both intimate and expansive — a table in a forest house on a rain-soaked night, one iteration among countless others.

The pendulum continued its arc. It might be possible that it marked more than one hour at once.

Sledzie shimmered in candlelight as if briefly alive. Black tea was poured into thick cups, steam rising in pale spirals and dissolving into the room.

The evening spread. Soft sounds threaded through the room — wood shifted, porcelain sighed, fire crackled, and voices lingered in the air. Somewhere beyond it, centuries of evenings, tables, and laughter seemed to ripple like folds of dark silk, threading into the long, dark fabric of time.

The old clock marked the hour with a low, severe chime. It was time to leave. We rose, chairs scraped across tile, a sound too sharp for the hour. Coats were lifted. Scarves were wrapped. Kisses exchanged. The dog followed us to the door, his white body briefly luminous in the spill of interior light.

The fog hung low over the track, softening puddles and trees alike. Headlights traced wavering paths, the car rocking gently over the uneven ground. The forest pressed close, folding itself around us once more. Wipers moved in steady arcs.

My sister scrolled on her phone, stifling a yawn. My father drove carefully, guided as much by memory as by sight. Inside the car, the faint scent of smoke lingered on our coats.

The forest and the night might have swallowed the road entirely, keeping the house, the stories, the candles, all folded, preserved in the dark.

It felt less like departure than adjustment — as though we had stepped slightly to one side. It would not have surprised me if, once we were out of sight, the house returned to its posture, the candles straightening, the clock resuming its private conversation with the walls.

Winter nights do this — they tangle everything, loosen the seams of time, dissolving the borders between the living and the gone, the past and the present.

As we drove forward, the sensation remained — not of depth beneath us, but of something running alongside. Another current, quiet and parallel. The rain tapped the roof in delicate rhythm. My sister's soft laugh broke through the sound of the wipers. Strange and inhabited, the night went on shimmering.
