I was thirteen and driving the American West, absent from school and all its silent choreography — its invisible lines, its measuring of minutes, its insistence that life be folded into something neat and quantifiable. No attendance register. No permission slips. No clock dividing me into sanctioned portions. Somewhere overseas, children my age were moving from room to room like pieces on a board, instructed when to speak, when to sit, when to imagine. We drove west into open country, the land stripped by winter of ornament. What remained was bone and clarity.

![](/assets/images/IMG_8162.JPG)

The earth lay open, austere. Fences swallowed by drift. Telegraph poles thinning into vanishing points. Cattle standing against white like punctuation — immovable, unreadable. Above, the sky yawned like a cold eye, unmoved by everything below it. Wind crossed the plains without concern, reshaping snow and dust with the same indifference.

I spent most hours in the rear seat, forehead against cold glass, watching the country unspool in horizontal bands — desert, pine forest, frozen river, red escarpment — each older than the idea of schooling, older than the idea of career. Towns appeared at dusk — a petrol station humming, a diner light against the dark, a church steeple alone against the sky, an American flag snapping in cold wind — then dissolved as if they had only imagined themselves into being.

At deserted National Park visitor centres, lonesome rangers shuffled maps behind counters. Stuffed coyotes and bighorn sheep and mountain lions stared out with permanent, unseeing eyes.

We lived motel to motel. Long corridors, thin walls, large American cars idling under fluorescence. Oatmeal in paper bowls. Cereal in plastic cups. Ice machines grinding through the night. Mornings we repacked and drove again. My mother steady at the wheel. My brother drifting in and out of sleep. Tyres on asphalt, low and insistent. Diesel. Sagebrush. Snowmelt. The metallic scent of cold air through a half-open window. The road didn't ask who I intended to become. It only stretched forward.

![](/assets/images/IMG_8166.JPG)

The farther west we went, the less civilisation felt substantial. A thin arrangement — effective, maybe, but fragile. Out there, consequence returned to its actual scale. Somewhere in that repetition — the miles, the wind, the exposure — I felt something in me widen. As if the horizon were recalibrating something interior I hadn't known was off.

School had always felt like rehearsal for a life already drafted. At thirteen I didn't have the vocabulary for systems. I only felt their compression. We learned to read. We learned to behave. We learned to avoid embarrassment. Ambitions scaled to fit available forms. Actor. Astronaut. Olympic champion. Say it too earnestly and adults would smile with careful mercy: be realistic. I was good at school. I memorised what they asked and returned it cleanly. Imagination trimmed to fit the margins. School didn't crush me. It compressed me. And like most children, I began to collaborate with the compression — rounding off edges, reducing the amplitude of wanting, learning to pre-fail quietly so I wouldn't fail visibly. Dreams should be proportionate to probability. Excess invites correction.

Out there, that felt absurd. The land held its own measure. Dunes rose like frozen waves. Deep canyons yawned into vertigo, cliffs bleeding copper into twilight. Spires of stone jutted from the earth like congregations frozen mid-prayer. Snow pressed into hollows. Ice clung to cactus and juniper. Tracks — human, elk, coyote — appeared and vanished. Ghost towns stood like memory made stone, windows dark and empty. Ravens coasted low. Coyotes called from unseen washes, voices folding into hollows. Night came early; stars pricked the sky through cold, still air. Somewhere, a low ridge shimmered under half-moon light, and a distant canyon lip vanished into shadow as if it had never existed. Water had carved stone through persistence alone. The mountains didn't bow or bend, didn't seek witness or approval. The land didn't explain itself. It didn't submit to evaluation. It didn't ask whether its scale was appropriate.

![](/assets/images/IMG_8163.JPG)

Standing there — a thirteen-year-old with wind pressing against my ribs — I felt small, but not reduced. In those parks we were often alone. Not metaphorically. Literally alone. No audience. No supervision. I could hear my own breath in the cold, feel my pulse in my throat. No one grading the moment. The air tasted mineral and ancient. For a few minutes at a time, I could almost believe I belonged more to that emptiness than to any classroom across an ocean. The land didn't affirm me. It didn't console me. Its indifference was absolute. And that indifference steadied me.

It occurred to me — not as rebellion, not as ideology, just as fact — that much of what governs us is constructed from mutual agreement and repetition. Bells ring because we agree they matter. Timetables hold because we comply. Futures are diagrammed because enough people believe in the diagram. Systems are useful fictions. They organise traffic. They process bodies. But they are not ultimate.

The desert didn't care about arrangements. We were asked what we would become before we'd figured out who we were. No time allotted for bewilderment. At thirteen, it felt like someone had decided the outcome before I'd had a chance to test the edges.

It is a strange thing — to exist on a planet that conjures auroras above frozen wastelands, glaciers grinding mountains into rivers, geysers spouting fire into still skies, volcanoes venting ash into violet air — and to be asked, in the same breath, to be realistic. To compress yourself into tidy, imaginable forms. The world doesn't measure or approve or confine. Only we try to do that.

Somewhere between motel corridors and canyon depth, a quiet certainty settled in me: attempt the full extension of your capacities. Not because success is guaranteed — it isn't — but because self-diminution is a subtler form of disappearance. Most lives aren't destroyed by catastrophe. They are narrowed by the decision to become reasonable too early.

There is a version of me who complied. Who chose the safe path, translated ambition into practicality. She wouldn't be miserable. She'd function — efficiently, cleanly. She would not falter, but she would erode. Subtle, imperceptible, internal erosion — a life that never quite inhabited itself, hollowing out until the shape was exact and empty, beautiful only in the absence of what might have been.

When the trip ended and I went back to school, nothing visible had changed. Bells rang. Lessons resumed. Futures were discussed with administrative calm. But I could no longer believe the classroom was the primary arena of my becoming. I had seen a horizon that made ceilings look provisional. I had watched rivers fracture stone without credential. I had felt wind erase boundary lines overnight.

I didn't come back defiant. I came back unconvinced. No one is really watching. The world isn't keeping score. The fear that keeps most people small is largely imagined — reinforced by rooms that mistake their own authority for universality. Beyond those rooms, gravity operates without commentary. Weather continues without applause.

I got lucky. I got to leave and see more before the rules had finished shaping me. But I think often of how many never see a scale large enough to recalibrate them. How many compress themselves to fit projections. How many consent to reduction because they mistake predictability for safety.

What unsettled me most wasn't that the world was vast. It was that it was indifferent. Entire civilisations rise and vanish against those cliffs. Rivers carve through stone without memory of the stone they remove. Wind erases footprints in minutes. Nothing in that landscape suggested human intention carried special weight. The desert doesn't care if you fulfil your potential. The canyon doesn't grieve your compromise. The horizon never applauds, not even for courage.

And yet. Somewhere in that indifference, I recognised something almost severe in its generosity. If the world does not require you to become anything in particular, neither does it forbid you. What doesn't crown you also doesn't cage you. The absence of cosmic endorsement is also the absence of cosmic limitation.

The world is vast. It does not measure us, nor does it judge. It does not insist on meaning. It asks nothing of us, sets no standard, offers no guidance. And there is a hard freedom in knowing that.
