I have spent my life in motion. Not always geographically — sometimes only inwardly — but rarely held by a single place long enough to mistake it for permanence. Childhood stretched wide and porous. Nights accumulated in motels, yurts, ryokans — the smell of smoke, dogs yipping into the dark, heaters humming too hot, boots thick with mud. Titirangi, Ushguli, Karakol, Lofoten, Skagway — all these places registered less as destinations than as intervals. Travel was never leisure; it was the medium of my life.

France and Poland were the closest things to constants, though even home came in two languages. I had access to two worlds, which felt like a secret advantage. But I was always a little more French in Poland, a little more Polish in France. Each place brought the other into focus. There were things that worked in one language and fell flat in the other, things that were funny here and strange there. The world kept doubling itself. Nothing was entirely foreign, and nothing was entirely mine.

My brother used to call me *la sauvage*. He’d say it when I tangled French, Polish and English into some private language of my own. When I disappeared inside myself at parties. When I spent evenings listening to songs in languages I couldn’t speak. When I ate smoked zebra and crickets and fermented shark and enjoyed it. *La sauvage*. He meant it the way younger brothers mean things — to get under your skin, to find the word that sticks. It stuck.

When I was ten, in Iceland, we stopped to see Seljalandsfoss. Everyone was being stupid and annoying. I looked at the waterfall and then I looked at all of them and I walked away.

Seljalandsfoss in January had gone hard and mean — the ground locked under sheet ice, the air with teeth in it. There was a sign. Closed or dangerous, probably. I walked past it.

The path curved behind the waterfall and I followed it until the view opened and the cold hit me full in the face and I thought: good. The water came down beside me in a roar I could feel in my chest. I was alone in a way that had nothing to do with loneliness.

Then I turned around.

The slope was pure ice. The same slope I had walked up without thinking, carried by anger. I couldn’t walk down it, so I went on my hands and backside, cheeks stinging, the cold burning through my clothes. There was nothing to catch me if I slipped. The water was very far down and I was ten years old and completely alone and the world was enormous and did not care about me at all.

Eventually I made it down. I walked back to the bus, to my mother and all the rest of them. I didn’t say anything. Because I didn’t want to be scolded. And because it was mine. My whole body was shaking — that deep trembling after fear runs its course. Beneath the terror, something bright and animal and completely awake.

It didn’t cure me. In the Seychelles, my brother and I threw ourselves into waves strong enough to erase us. They rolled us, held us under, spat us back gasping, and we ran straight back in. In Namibia, we went down into the Fish River Canyon, rock to rock into the belly of the giant, past the carcasses of antelopes, and swam in the river at the bottom. The climb out was under full African sun. Somewhere in the middle my legs stopped working — not tired, done. At the top the staff were laughing. *We didn’t think you’d make it*, they said.

And in Kazakhstan, I rode for three days on a horse named Typhoon, who ignored me until I stopped forcing him. Then we found each other. The land was gold and rust and enormous. We galloped — the wind tearing the breath from my lungs — until there was nothing left but the land and the horse.

My mother used to scribble her favourite lines on our kitchen walls. One of them: *Le monde est un livre, et ceux qui ne voyagent pas n’en lisent qu’une page* — the world is a book, and those who do not travel read only one page. I believed it for a long time. I still half do. But a life doesn’t become interesting only when you leave home. Some of the pages I would miss most were the ones I read over and over, in the same handful of places, while I was too young to know they were finite.

In Poland, every summer, my grandfather drove my brother, my sister and me to his sister’s house. Back then, everyone was still alive. We sat beneath the oak tree in her yard, drinking tea, eating cake, playing ball, gossiping, laughing at things that probably weren’t very funny. The adults talked. The afternoon went on. Nothing happened that would have made a good story. I would give a great deal to sit there again.

Last winter, my brother and I started taking night walks. We’d wander through the empty village and out towards the fields, talking about our dreams, remembering things, setting the world to rights. Two people with plenty of opinions and, between us, presumably enough solutions for several continents.

There is something strange about growing up beside another person. You accumulate a private history, a whole catalogue of things nobody else would understand properly. And then one night, you find yourselves walking through the dark, discussing the future as if it were a place you might reach on foot. The road is black. Smoke climbs from the chimneys, blurring the stars. My brother is there. He still calls me *la sauvage*, sometimes. For now, that is enough.

And then there are all the people beyond that small circle. People whose lives briefly cross yours, sometimes for no reason at all. Everyone is carrying a world of their own — a history, a set of convictions, private absurdities, things they know and things they’ll never understand. You catch glimpses of it on a street, over a meal, in a conversation that lasts ten minutes. The stranger those worlds are to me, the more familiar humanity itself becomes.

I don’t know what to make of that, exactly. I only know that the world has repeatedly been stranger, harsher, funnier and more generous than I expected.

Once, arriving by bus in Vilnius at night, I was approached by two drunk men asking for a light. The sensible thing would have been to say I didn’t have one and keep walking. Instead, I gave them fire. They began speaking to me in very bad English, occasionally turning to Russian between themselves to find the words. I understood both languages and said nothing. As I was leaving, I called out, “Alright, пока-пока, guys.”

One of them stopped.<br>
“Эй! Ты говоришь по-русски?”<br>
“Not really, but I’m Polish.”<br>
And then one of them shouted, “*Wilno nasze!*” (Vilnius is ours!), the old Polish claim on the city.

There are more dignified ways to encounter the complicated history of a city. This was not one of them. But there we were, three people in the middle of the night, finding a common language in mischief, alcohol and a phrase nobody had particularly needed to hear that evening. I walked away laughing.

When we lived in Honfleur, our little cinema was usually a quiet affair, five or six people scattered through the dark. That evening, for the new *Star Wars*, it was packed. When the opening theme began, a group of men stood up, hands over their hearts.

It was so earnest it was almost funny — a roomful of grown men saluting fictional space politics. And yet. All these people had come together for a story, and whatever they had found in it was real enough to bring them to their feet.

And the world doesn’t stop at what happened. There are places I’ve never been except through books, films, songs, stories told in the dark. People who never existed and yet feel familiar: a girl on a wagon trail whose voice I still hear every time I see an open plain. I have lived inside other people’s imaginations for as long as I can remember, and carried pieces of them back into my own.

My grandfather used to tell us a story his own father had told him: one hot summer day, he had carried a calf on his back across the fields, and the calf was the devil, and the whole way home it tightened around his throat. Now, when we drive through the countryside around our town, past a field I decided, years ago, was the one, I catch myself looking. Sometimes I swear I see him.

Some days I can no longer tell what I remember, what I was told, and what I’ve made up along the way.

I have never been able to leave a place entirely behind. Long after the journey ends, pieces of it return without warning: a certain quality of light, a smell, a song heard somewhere far from home. They mingle with other things I have carried for just as long — my grandfather’s stories at dusk, my mother’s lines on the walls, names and old digicodes. The mind keeps what it wants, or what it can. The reasons are rarely clear.

The real seeps into the imagined; the imagined changes what we notice in the real. A memory acquires the atmosphere of a dream. A dream borrows the smell of a place we once passed through. Nothing stays quite as it was, and yet something remains.

Maybe that’s what all these years have left me with: not a collection of places, not a clean story about who I am, but a life full of overlapping worlds. The people I came from. The people I met briefly and never saw again.

And yet, none of it belongs entirely to the past. Somewhere, all of it is still happening. Smoke is rising above the village roofs. Someone is pouring tea beneath the oak tree. In a field I chose long ago, a calf is still being carried home. The waterfall keeps falling.

And what should I do with it all — the warmth, the gone, the dreamt and the road?
