title: The Curse of Tash Rabat

I have seen enough of this world to know it keeps its secrets. Not out of malice. But simply because it does not concern itself with being understood. The world is vast, old, and largely indifferent to the neat explanations we prefer to lay over it. I have always found that more comforting than frightening. It means there are still edges to things.

The story I am about to tell is about a place, and about a child, and about one of those nights when several small coincidences arrive together and leave you wondering what exactly you have just witnessed.

Make of it what you will.

We were driving south through Poland, four of us in a car headed towards the mountains. The forest pressed close on either side. Sunlight fell in thin, trembling ribbons, gold and ghostly. Frost fractured it, throwing the world into splinters. Beyond, the fields stretched white and empty, sky and earth bleeding into each other until there was no horizon, only white dissolving into white.

My brother, Arthur, sat beside me in the back seat, a pair of skis wedged between us. We were peeling mandarins. The smell cut sharply through the sealed warmth of the car. The road unspooled south.

We had been in the car long enough for conversation to run dry, and we had started inventing ways to pass the time.

I suggested a question: what is the strangest, most unexplainable thing that has ever happened to you? Arthur answered almost immediately. Probably the fairies I saw in the yurt in Kyrgyzstan.

The word sat wrong. It did not belong to him. He said it casually, the way someone notes a small irregularity in an otherwise ordinary story. I turned and looked at him. He looked back, those dark blue eyes carrying something old and unresolved. What do you mean? I asked. In the second yurt camp, he said. The night I was sick.

I remembered that night. Or rather, I remembered what I thought had been the whole of it. It turned out I had only ever known the outside.

Years earlier, that summer, we had been travelling across Kyrgyzstan. We had left the plains around Song-Kol Lake behind, driving for hours through the high interior of the country before climbing steadily into the At-Bashy range. The road deteriorated with altitude. Asphalt into gravel, gravel into dirt, the car rocking over ruts and loose stone while the horizon folded inward, the sky enormous above us.

By mid-afternoon we reached a 3,300m valley. The yurt camp sat low in the grasslands. A narrow river cut quickly through the valley floor, fed by ice high above. Eagles circled slowly overhead. Herds of horses drifted across distant hillsides, dark shapes in golden grass. The land hummed.

I have been to wild places. But Kyrgyzstan at altitude feels genuinely remote in a way that is difficult to describe from the outside. It was the kind of country that made you feel both very small and very awake, and I loved it with my whole ribcage.

The wind tangled my hair. The sun on my face. Fresh air and wild grass stung my lungs. Mountains rose around me. Everything felt possible. I was untethered.

I was twelve that summer. Arthur was eight and completely feral with it. Hair wild from the wind, untamed, entirely at home in his own fearlessness, with that particular restlessness that makes adults nervous. He was the kind of child who heard the word danger and understood it as a direction.

The yurts were small, round, and warm inside. Thick felt panels in deep reds and burnt oranges layered the walls, geometric patterns repeating like a logic you almost grasp. It smelled of wool, smoke, animal, and something much older.

Nearby stood Tash Rabat.

It did not so much sit in the landscape as emerge from it. A low stone structure the same grey-brown colour as the surrounding mountains, so precisely matched to its environment that it seemed less constructed than revealed, as though the hillside had always held it there and one day simply allowed it to surface. Low, rectangular, squat. No towers. No ornament. No invitation. A building that appeared almost reluctant to be found.

We walked towards it in the gold-smeared afternoon, our guide explaining that it had once served travellers along the Silk Road. Merchants, caravans, centuries of passage through the mountains. But I was only half-listening; the land spoke louder to me. Then she paused.

No one knows exactly how many rooms there are inside. Some say thirty. Others say thirty-one. That struck me immediately as a solvable problem. Well, can we not just count them? I asked. You could, she said. But if you count the rooms, you will get cursed. Now I was all ears. What kind of curse? She shrugged. I don't know. You just get cursed. The answer landed and stayed there.

The entrance faced east. Crossing it, the temperature dropped as if the stone had been storing cold for centuries and finally released it. Inside, the light nearly failed. Corridors branched in several directions, each narrowing as it went, leading into rooms with ceilings so low you had to crouch. You could not always tell how far you had gone or which way you were facing. The place seemed to quietly rearrange itself each time you turned. Like it had its own logic, like certain dreams do.

Officially, Tash Rabat is a caravanserai. A stone waystation for Silk Road merchants. But it does not resemble most caravanserais. Too remote, too enclosed, too dark. Some historians believe it was a monastery before any of that. Local stories speak of underground chambers, secret tunnels, hidden rooms that do not appear on any record. The architecture accommodates every theory without confirming any, and seems content to keep doing so.

When Arthur heard the word cursed, his eyes lit up like a dare had been issued. Being cursed, at eight, in a stone fortress in the mountains of central Asia, sounded less like a warning than an invitation. He began counting as we moved through the corridors together, his voice bouncing off the walls, serious and delighted, pushing ahead into darker passages where the light no longer reached. The numbers climbed. Then stopped. Corridors looped back. Rooms appeared where he was sure he had already counted. He started over. Then again. Eventually his voice faded. Children have a way of abandoning problems the moment something else captures their attention, and somewhere in that stone labyrinth, he simply forgot about the count. The number remained unfinished. Left somewhere in the dark.

We stepped outside into the last of the afternoon light. The valley reasserted itself. The horses had not moved. Nothing had changed.

That night, the camp settled into stillness. Dinner in the main yurt. Warm bread, tea, bowls of something thick and nourishing. The stove ticked. Outside, the mountains had disappeared into darkness, their presence implied only by the way the stars stopped at a certain point and left a void.

After dinner, Arthur was put to bed in our yurt. Our grandmother kept quiet company beside him. My mother and I walked to the latrines at the edge of the camp. Outside, the night was dense and unusually still. Not the softened dark of cities, but the original dark. Smoke rose in a straight line from the main yurt. A dog barked once somewhere and stopped.

When we came back, Arthur was already asleep. We settled in. Lying in the yurt you heard the river and the wind and nothing else.

The episode happened later, deep in the night.

He woke suddenly, screaming. Not the confused crying of a child half out of sleep, but something sharper, more frantic. He was shouting nonsense, telling our grandmother not to cut him with her axe, his voice high and desperate in the dark. Pure terror pressed on him, unmoored from reason.

He scrambled out of the bed and ran towards the door as if the room itself had turned against him. The darkness pressed close, heavy and alive, and he vomited where he fell. And then nothing. Silence folded over the valley, as though the night had never opened.

The screaming ended. The panic drained out of him as abruptly as it had come. He went back to bed and fell asleep again, breathing slow and even, as though nothing unusual had happened at all.

I barely registered it. It was the kind of thing that happens in the night that the night absorbs.

By morning he seemed perfectly normal. Hungry, energetic, those dark blue eyes clear and present as ever. No fever. No lingering sickness. No memory of anything unusual.

At least, that was my version. What I had seen from the outside.

Five years later, in a car filled with winter sunlight and the smell of mandarins, he told us what had happened on the inside.

In the middle of the night, he woke. Above him, in the open space of the yurt, small winged figures moved through the air. Not at the edge of vision. Not the uncertain shapes of dreams. Directly above him.

They were very small with thin translucent wings like dragonflies. They moved quickly, weaving through the air in tight restless patterns, absorbed in something urgent among themselves. And they were speaking. Not words exactly. More like layered whispers vibrating just beneath meaning, a language your mind cannot hold. He watched them the way you watch something that is simply there. They seemed dimly aware of him, the way you are aware of a stone in a field. Registered, but irrelevant.

He was eight, in the dark, in a valley far from home, and there were things in the air of that yurt that should not exist. He knew it the way you know certain things without being taught. That this was wrong, that the world was not supposed to look like this. The fear came then, total and immediate, swallowing the room. The stove's glow, the felt walls, the dark beyond the doorway, even the presence of our grandmother. It pressed on him, filling every inch. After that, memory fractured. Nothing beyond the figures, and the feeling of having glimpsed a seam in the world that should never have been seen.

When he finished, something moved along my skin. A thrill.

We tried to take it apart the way you take apart anything that refuses to make sense. Altitude sickness was the first candidate. Hypoxia can produce hallucinations vivid enough to feel real, confusion, sudden panic. Food poisoning was possible. A brief fever, delirium, something caught in the body and released. All of it plausible. None of it sufficient.

What stayed with me was the word he had used. Fairies. What had he actually seen?

He described them again. Small, winged, fast, their voices like whispers. And something in my memory shifted. Kyrgyzstan is a Muslim country. Djinn are not metaphors there. Small beings, peripheral, largely indifferent to humans, said to inhabit desolate and liminal spaces.

One thought cut through the others. Tash Rabat. The curse. The rooms that should not be counted. The number my brother had begun and never finished.

What a strange coincidence. A child wandering through ancient halls, a count abandoned in the dark, and in the middle of the night tiny winged figures arguing above his bed. We sat with it. No one claimed it explained anything.

Maybe it was nothing. Maybe it was everything. The not-knowing was the best part. All I knew was that I wanted more of it. More strangeness, more cracks in the ordinary, more of whatever this world keeps hidden just out of sight.

The light through the pines was very white. My brother peeled another mandarin. Soon we would be in the mountains, lungs full of cold air, skis under our feet, the world simple and fast and good. But not yet. For now, the world sat quiet around us, enormous and mostly unknown. How thrilling is that.
