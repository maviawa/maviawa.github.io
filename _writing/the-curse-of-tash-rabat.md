The story I’m about to tell is about a place, a curse, a kid, and one night in the mountains that none of us has ever managed to explain. 

Make of it what you will.

We were driving south through Poland towards the mountains, my father and my sister up front. The forest pressed close on either side. Sunlight fell in thin, trembling ribbons, gold and ghostly. Frost fractured it, throwing the world into splinters.

My brother, Arthur, sat beside me in the back seat, a pair of skis wedged between us. We were peeling mandarins. The smell cut sharply through the sealed warmth of the car.

We had been in the car long enough for conversation to run dry, and we had started inventing ways to pass the time.

I suggested a question:<br>
What’s the strangest, most unexplainable thing that has ever happened to you?<br>
Arthur answered almost immediately.<br>
Probably the fairies I saw in the yurt in Kyrgyzstan.

Fairies? Arthur isn’t someone who says *fairies*. But he wasn’t joking. He said it the way you’d mention the weather, and when I turned to look at him, his dark blue eyes held something old and unresolved.

What do you mean? I asked.<br>
In the second yurt camp, he said. The night I was sick.

I remembered that night. Or rather, I remembered what I thought had been the whole of it.

Five summers earlier, we — my mother, grandmother, brother and I — had been travelling across Kyrgyzstan. We had left the plains around Song-Köl Lake behind, driving for hours through the high interior of the country before climbing steadily into the At-Bashy Range. The road deteriorated with altitude — asphalt into gravel, gravel into dirt, the car rocking over ruts and loose stone while the horizon folded inward.

![](/assets/images/IMG_6869.JPG)

By mid-afternoon we reached a valley at 3,300 metres. The yurt camp sat low in the grasslands. A narrow river cut quickly through the valley floor, fed by ice high above. Eagles circled slowly overhead. Herds of horses drifted across distant hillsides, dark shapes in golden grass. The land hummed.

I climbed out of the car stiff from the road and breathed, and the air came into me cold off the ice, clean as creek water, air no city had ever touched, and it stung. I loved that place with my whole ribcage.

I was twelve that summer. Arthur was eight and completely feral with it, hair wild from the wind, never once still, the sort of restless that makes grown-ups nervous. He was the kind of kid who heard the word *danger* and understood it as a direction.

![](/assets/images/IMG_6859.JPG)

![](/assets/images/IMG_6868.JPG)

The yurts were small, round, and warm inside — thick felt panels in deep reds and burnt oranges layered the walls, geometric patterns repeating like a logic you almost grasp. It smelled of wool, smoke, animal and the mountains.

Nearby stood Tash Rabat.

It did not so much sit in the landscape as emerge from it — a low stone structure the same grey-brown colour as the surrounding mountains, so precisely matched to its environment that it seemed less constructed than revealed, as though the hillside had always held it there and one day simply allowed it to surface. Rectangular, squat. No towers. No ornament. No invitation. A building that appeared almost reluctant to be found.

![](/assets/images/IMG_6861.JPG)

We walked towards it in the gold-smeared afternoon, our guide explaining that it had once been a caravanserai, a stone waystation for Silk Road merchants, though some historians think it may have been a monastery long before that. And local stories speak of underground chambers, secret tunnels and hidden rooms that don’t appear on any record. But I was only half-listening; the land spoke louder to me. Then she said something that got my full attention.

No one knows exactly how many rooms there are inside. Some say thirty. Others say thirty-one.<br>
That struck me immediately as a solvable problem.<br>
Well, can’t we just count them? I said.<br>
You could, she said. But if you count the rooms, you’ll get cursed.<br>
What kind of curse? I asked.<br>
She shrugged. I don’t know. You just get cursed.<br>
The answer landed and stayed there.

When Arthur heard the word *cursed*, his eyes lit up like a dare had been issued. Being cursed, at eight, in a stone fortress somewhere in the mountains of Central Asia, sounded less like a warning than an invitation. By the time I looked round, he was already inside.

The entrance faced east. We stepped through it and the cold came out of the walls to meet us, the kind of cold that had been kept in the dark a long time. Inside, the light nearly failed, and it wasn’t hard to understand where the stories came from. Corridors branched in several directions, each narrowing as it went, leading into rooms with ceilings so low you had to crouch. You couldn’t always tell how far you’d gone or which way you were facing. The place seemed to quietly rearrange itself each time you turned, like it had its own logic, the way certain dreams do.

Arthur began counting as we moved deeper in, his voice bouncing off the walls, serious and delighted, as he pushed ahead into darker passages where the light never reached. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. The numbers climbed, then stalled. Passages looped back on themselves. Rooms turned up where he was sure he’d already counted. He started over. Then again. Then something else caught his eye, and the counting stopped the way things stop when you’re eight. The number stayed unfinished. Left in the dark.

We stepped out into the last of the light. The horses were still grazing where we’d left them. As far as anyone could tell, nothing had changed.

![](/assets/images/IMG_6864.JPG)

That night, the camp settled into stillness. Dinner in the main yurt — warm bread, tea, bowls of something thick. The stove ticked. Outside, the mountains had disappeared into darkness, their presence implied only by the way the stars stopped at a certain point and left a void.

After dinner, Arthur was put to bed in our yurt. Our grandmother kept quiet company beside him. My mother and I walked to the edge of the camp. Out there, the night was the original dark, deep and very still. Smoke rose in a straight line from the main yurt. A dog barked once somewhere and stopped.

When we came back, Arthur was already asleep. We settled in. Lying in the yurt you heard the river and the wind and nothing else.

The episode came later, deep in the night. I was half asleep, and I remember it in pieces. Arthur woke screaming — not the confused crying of a child waking from a bad dream, but something sharper, more frantic. He was shouting at our grandmother, or so I thought then — *don’t cut me, don’t cut me with your axe* — his voice high and desperate in the dark. There was no axe. There had never been an axe. He scrambled out of bed and ran for the door as if the yurt itself had turned against him, and he vomited where he fell.

Then it stopped. The panic drained out of him as abruptly as it had come. He went back to bed and slept, breathing slow and even, as though nothing had happened at all.

Some things happen in the night, and the night absorbs them.

By morning he seemed perfectly normal — hungry, energetic, those dark blue eyes clear and present as ever. No fever. No lingering sickness. No mention of anything unusual.

At least, that was my version. What I had seen from the outside.

![](/assets/images/IMG_6865.JPG)

Five years later, in the back of a car in Poland, he told us what had happened on the inside.

In the middle of the night, he woke. Above him, in the open space of the yurt, small winged figures moved through the air. Not at the edge of vision. Not the uncertain shapes of dreams. Directly above him.

They were very small with thin translucent wings like dragonflies. They moved quickly, weaving through the air in tight restless patterns, absorbed in something urgent among themselves. And they were speaking. Not words, exactly — more like layered whispers vibrating just beneath meaning, a language your mind can’t hold. He watched them the way you watch something that is simply there. They seemed dimly aware of him, the way you are aware of a stone in a field. Registered, but irrelevant.

He was eight, in the dark, in a valley far from home, and there were things in the air of that yurt that should not exist. He knew — not in his head but in his skin — that this was wrong, that a door had been left open that was meant to stay shut. Then the fear came, all at once and from everywhere, and it ate the room: the stove’s glow, the felt walls, the dark beyond the doorway, until there was nothing left but him and the whispering above his bed. He couldn’t breathe around it. He couldn’t think past it.

After that, memory fractured. He didn’t remember shouting, or being sick. Only the figures, and the feeling of having glimpsed a seam in the world he was never meant to see.

When he finished, something moved along my skin. A thrill.

We tried to take it apart the way you take apart anything that refuses to make sense. My sister asked about timing. My father asked what he’d eaten. Altitude sickness was the first candidate — hypoxia can produce hallucinations vivid enough to feel real, confusion, sudden panic. But we had stayed at that height, and by morning he was hungry and wild as ever. Food poisoning doesn’t end with one night of being sick. A fever would have left its mark, and there had been none. Delirium, then — but delirium from what? And why that night, of all nights?

I kept coming back to his word. *Fairies*.

He described them again — small, winged, fast, their voices like whispers — and another word surfaced from somewhere deep in my mind. *Jinn*.

Kyrgyzstan is a mostly Muslim country, and a girl at my school had once spoken of them in a hushed voice, as if they might overhear. What she described was almost exactly what Arthur had seen. They kept to desolate places, she said. Caves and ruins. Not all of them meant harm. But some liked to whisper to people, and some could slip inside a body and turn it against itself — a sudden change, a fit, speaking in voices not their own, a sickness no one could explain.

Put side by side, it read like the start of a ghost story. A curse. A count abandoned in the dark. And that same night, a terror with no cause, a boy sick on the yurt floor, and tiny winged figures arguing above his bed. We sat with it. No one claimed it explained anything.

Maybe it was the altitude after all. Maybe it was something older. I didn’t need to know. I liked that there were still things in this world nobody had an answer for, and I wanted more of them — more strangeness, more cracks in the ordinary, more of whatever is kept just out of sight.

The light through the pines was very white. My brother peeled another mandarin. Soon we would be in the mountains, lungs full of cold air, skis under our feet, everything simple and fast and good. But not yet. For now, the world sat quiet around us, keeping its own counsel. And far away, in the dark rooms of Tash Rabat, a count is still waiting to be finished.
