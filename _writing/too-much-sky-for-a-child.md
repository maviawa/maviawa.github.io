I was thirteen and road-tripping across the American West, absent from school and all its silent choreography — its invisible lines, its measuring of minutes, its insistence that life be folded into something neat and quantifiable. Other mothers signed permission slips. Mine took us out for four months and halfway around the world. America, New Zealand, Australia, Japan. We missed lessons. We learned the geography of departure — airports before dawn, roads we couldn’t read, the strange intimacy of being nowhere near home. I think she knew the world had things to teach us that school never would.

Somewhere across the ocean, children my age were moving from room to room like pieces on a board, told when to speak, how to sit, what to imagine. School never crushed me. It just made me smaller, a little at a time, and taught me to call that being realistic. Dreams were meant to be proportionate to probability. Keep at that long enough and you end up with a life lived adjacent to itself.

We drove west into open country, the land stripped bare by winter. What remained was bone and clarity.

![](/assets/images/IMG_8162.JPG)

Fences swallowed by drift. Telegraph poles thinning into nothing. Cattle standing against the white like punctuation, still as stones and unreadable. And above it all, the sky yawned like a cold eye.

The country kept changing its face. Desert gave way to pine, pine to frozen river, river to red rock that seemed to burn even under cloud. Snow lay blue in the shadows of the mountains. Towns came up out of the dusk — a petrol station humming, a diner light flickering, a church steeple alone against the dark — and then they were gone, like they had only dreamt themselves up.

My mother did all the driving. At home we didn’t even own a car. Out here she drove for weeks, across half a continent, Joan Baez and the Eagles on the stereo and two children in the back. In Durango, she somehow ended up driving on the left side of the road. It became the running joke of the trip.

We lived from motel to motel. Long corridors, thin walls, rooms stuck somewhere between 1955 and 1975, a little soulless, a little creepy. The carpets had seen better decades. Big American cars idled under fluorescent light, and all night the ice machines chewed away. In the mornings we ate oatmeal out of paper bowls, packed the car and left. Diesel. Sagebrush. Snowmelt. Cold air pulled through a half-open window.

At deserted visitor centres, lonesome rangers shuffled maps behind counters. Stuffed bighorn sheep and elk and mountain lions stared out with glass eyes, dead and labelled. Outside, the parking lots were empty, and the real thing was waiting beyond them.

We walked till the cold got into our boots. We climbed whatever could be climbed, balanced along fallen trees, crossed bridges that were barely more than planks. The ground was frozen in places and loose in others, and there was always a path going round a bend, and we always followed it. We came back with numb fingers and pockets full of things that would mean nothing to anyone else.

Once, my brother lost a shoe in a huge pile of snow. I laughed through the whole search. He stood there in one wet sock and didn’t laugh at all. All that snow, all that country, and the three of us on our knees digging for one shoe.

We walked under trees that had outlived most of what we learned in history lessons, stood beside rock that looked carved by hand, came to the edges of canyons so deep the river at the bottom made no sound. Standing there, wind pushing at my ribs, I felt small. But small is not the same as less. I could see my breath, feel my pulse knocking in my throat. And for a while I belonged more to that vastness than to any classroom across any ocean.

The land did not comfort me. It didn’t know I was there. And somehow that was the steadiest thing I had ever felt.

In Monument Valley, a Diné man led us out on horseback, his two dogs trotting at the horses’ heels. He called the valley Tsé Biiʼ Ndzisgaii. A textbook would call the shapes rising around us buttes and mesas. He spoke of ancient spirits and deities. I don’t remember everything he told us. But I remember feeling that there was more than one way to understand the world.

![](/assets/images/IMG_8166.JPG)

Time loosened its grip. The days ran into one another the way creeks run into rivers, without asking. There was always somewhere to go and nowhere we had to be. The land changed outside the window. For once the future could wait, and it did, and nothing bad came of it.

When the trip ended, months later, and I went back to school, nothing had changed. Bells rang. Lessons resumed. But I could no longer believe the classroom was the primary arena of my becoming. I had seen a horizon that made ceilings look provisional.

I did not come back defiant. I came back unconvinced. And slightly bored.

It became clear that no one was really watching. Most of what governs us is built from agreement and repetition. Bells ring because we agree they matter. Timetables hold because we comply with them. We inherit a set of arrangements and mistake their authority for something universal. The world is not keeping score.

Many lives are not destroyed by catastrophe. They are narrowed by quiet resignation. By the decision to become reasonable too early. The erosion is almost imperceptible, like wind working on sandstone, until the shape of a life is exact, and empty.

But the world is indifferent. Out there, nothing suggested that human intention carried any special weight. Wind takes your footprints before you’ve turned around. The desert doesn’t care if you live up to your potential, and the horizon never applauds. Not even for courage.

That ought to be a cold thing to know. Yet there is a generosity in it so plain it’s almost severe. If the world doesn’t need you to become anything in particular, then it can’t forbid you either. Nothing out there was cheering me on. Nothing out there was stopping me.

Only people build fences. And I had seen what the snow does to them.

There is a hard freedom in knowing that.
